# Should you build on beehiiv? (Beginner Guide)

If you've never written a line of code and you're just trying to decide where to put your newsletter, this guide is for you. I'm going to walk through what beehiiv actually is, what it costs in plain dollars, and how it stacks up against Substack — the other name you've probably heard. This sits inside a bigger project I'm building around one idea: don't let a platform own your audience. Every tool I evaluate, I'm asking the same question — if this company disappeared tomorrow, could you walk away with your list and your writing intact? beehiiv does better on that question than most, but "better than most" still isn't the same as "yours."

## What beehiiv actually is

beehiiv is a website where you write and send email newsletters. Think of it as three things bolted together: an email-sending tool, a simple website for your archive, and (if you want it) a way to charge readers a subscription fee. You type a post in their editor, hit send, and it goes to everyone on your list as an email and also gets posted as a page on your own beehiiv-hosted site. It was built by a former Morning Brew engineering lead, and it's explicitly positioned as the "we do this better" alternative to Substack.

## What it costs, in plain terms

beehiiv has four plans. Here's what each one actually gets you, translated out of marketing language:

- **Launch — $0/month.** Free forever, no credit card needed, for up to 2,500 subscribers.
  - Unlimited sends
  - Your own custom domain
  - A website
  - A podcast feed if you want one
  - What you *can't* do: charge anyone money — no paid subscriptions, no ads, no monetization tools at all
- **Scale — starts around $43/month (billed yearly) or $49/month (billed monthly)** for your first 1,000 subscribers, climbing as your list grows, up to $329/month around 100,000 subscribers. This is the plan that unlocks money-making:
  - Paid subscriptions
  - An ads marketplace
  - Automated email sequences
  - Surveys
  - **0% platform fee on your subscription revenue** — you still pay Stripe's normal card-processing fee (2.9% + $0.30 per transaction), but beehiiv itself doesn't skim anything off your subscription income
- **Max — starts around $96/month (yearly) or $109/month (monthly),** rising to roughly $419/month at high subscriber counts.
  - Removes beehiiv's own branding from your site and emails
  - Adds a storefront for selling sponsorships directly
  - Unlimited team seats
  - Support for up to 10 separate newsletters under one account
- **Enterprise — custom pricing**, aimed at big media companies, with dedicated support and infrastructure most solo writers will never need.

That 0% fee on paid subscriptions is the single biggest reason people switch to beehiiv from Substack. Substack takes a flat 10% of every dollar you make from paid subscribers, on top of the same Stripe processing fee. If you're making real subscription revenue — say $5,000 a month — that's $500/month Substack keeps that beehiiv wouldn't.

## What "0% platform fee" really means (and doesn't mean)

This is the part beginners get tripped up on, so let me spell it out. "0% platform fee" means beehiiv, the company, does not take a cut of the subscription price your readers pay you. It does *not* mean the transaction is free — every credit card payment anywhere on the internet passes through a payment processor, and that processor charges its own fee no matter which newsletter platform you use.

Here's the same $10/month subscription, broken down on each platform:

- **On beehiiv**: Stripe takes about 59 cents (2.9% + $0.30), beehiiv takes nothing. You keep roughly $9.41.
- **On Substack**: Stripe takes the same 59 cents, plus Substack's 10% cut takes another dollar. You keep roughly $8.41.

That gap only matters once you're actually charging for subscriptions — if you're on the free Launch plan and not monetizing, it's a moot point.

## Where beehiiv beats Substack — and where it doesn't

Here's how I'd weigh the two, boiled down to what actually changes your decision:

- **Pick beehiiv if you plan to eventually charge for your newsletter.** The fee math favors you as you grow, and it only gets better the bigger your subscription revenue gets.
- **Pick beehiiv if you might want to move your content programmatically later.** It has a genuine API (more on that in the intermediate and expert guides), which is rare for a tool this size.
- **Stick with Substack if your growth plan depends on its "Discover" network.** That built-in recommendation feed can hand you free readers just by existing on the platform, and beehiiv doesn't have an equivalent shared discovery feed the same way.

If your audience strategy leans on Substack's internal recommendations, know that switching means trading that away.

## Data governance: how beehiiv actually handles your data

"Data governance" is just a fancy way of asking: who controls your information, what do they actually do with it, and what happens to it if something changes? I read beehiiv's privacy policy directly (it was last updated April 30, 2026) so you don't have to. Here's what it actually says, in plain language:

| Question | What beehiiv's policy actually says |
|---|---|
| Does beehiiv sell or share my data with advertisers? | Yes, in the technical sense privacy law uses. beehiiv's own policy states it discloses identifiers (name, email, IP address) and browsing activity to "advertising partners and analytics providers" — under California's privacy law this counts as a "sale" or "share" even though no money changes hands. You can opt out by emailing beehiiv directly or turning on a browser privacy signal called Global Privacy Control. |
| Is beehiiv GDPR compliant? | For your subscribers' data, beehiiv signs a Data Protection Addendum that includes Standard Contractual Clauses — the standard legal paperwork for moving European data to a U.S. company. beehiiv also earned a security certification called SOC 2 Type 1 in October 2025. That's evidence of real compliance infrastructure, not a guarantee — it means beehiiv has the paperwork serious platforms are expected to have. |
| What happens to my subscriber data if I close my publication? | It's deleted, and beehiiv is explicit that this is irreversible. Deleting a publication wipes its subscribers, segments, and associated data permanently — there's no recovery window. Export your list before you delete anything, not after. |
| What happens to my data if beehiiv itself is acquired? | beehiiv's own privacy policy answers this: your data can be transferred "to a buyer or other successor" in a merger, sale, or bankruptcy. Not hypothetical — beehiiv has raised roughly $50 million and was valued around $225 million as of 2026, real acquisition territory for a company that size. Their contract already tells you what happens: whoever buys beehiiv inherits your data along with everything else. |

The plain-language takeaway: beehiiv isn't secretly selling your data to shady buyers — it discloses the advertising-sharing piece plainly and gives you a way to opt out. But you're still trusting a small, private company with your subscriber relationship, and its own privacy policy already tells you that relationship is transferable if the company changes hands.

## Managed cloud vs. renting your own server: what leaving beehiiv actually means

If you ever do decide to leave beehiiv, "leaving" isn't one decision — it's a choice between fundamentally different ways of running a newsletter, each with real tradeoffs:

| | Staying on beehiiv | Self-hosted (e.g. Ghost on a rented server) | A managed-but-open stack (e.g. Vercel + Supabase) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who patches security updates | beehiiv, automatically — you never think about it | You do — it's your server, your responsibility | The hosting company patches the servers; you maintain your own code |
| Who's on call if it goes down | beehiiv's support team | You, unless you pay someone to be on call | The hosting company handles infrastructure outages; bugs in your own code are on you |
| Cost predictability | Fixed, tiered pricing you can see in advance | A flat monthly server bill, though a mistake can cost you real time or money to fix | Usage-based — cheap starting out, but can grow as traffic grows |
| Data portability | Limited — CSV exports work, but everything lives inside beehiiv's format until you export it | Total — it's a plain server you can move anywhere | Good — it's your own database, though you're still working inside that platform's deploy system |
| Technical skill required | None — this is the entire appeal of beehiiv | High — you're acting as your own system administrator | Moderate — mostly point-and-click, some comfort with code helps |

In plain terms: beehiiv trades control for convenience — a small, private company runs everything, and you accept that its terms and ownership could change under you. Self-hosting something like Ghost on a rented server (I walk through the exact steps in my Hetzner self-hosted Postgres guide) is the opposite trade: total control, total responsibility. A stack like Vercel + Supabase (covered in my separate deploy guide) sits in between — still hosted by companies, but ones you're not locked into the same way, since your own code talks to beehiiv's API rather than living entirely inside it. If you decide to actually move off beehiiv rather than just building alongside it, I walk through the real mechanics — content, subscribers, and paid billing — in my separate migration guide.

## Who should actually stay on beehiiv

I don't want this guide to read like everyone should be plotting their exit. Plenty of people shouldn't move right now, and I want to be honest about who I mean:

- **You already left something worse.** If you moved to beehiiv specifically because Substack's fees or "Discover" politics bothered you, you've already made the ownership-conscious choice. beehiiv's 0% subscription fee and genuine API mean you're not stuck the way you were before — you don't need to chase "more ownership" further just because it exists in theory.
- **Your list is small.** If you have a few hundred subscribers and no paid revenue, the effort of any migration — even a simple one — costs you more time than staying put costs you in risk. Revisit the decision once you're actually making money or your list gets big enough that switching later gets harder, not easier.
- **The 0% fee is worth the risk to you.** If you're making meaningful subscription revenue, beehiiv keeping 0% of it (versus Substack's 10%) may be worth accepting that a small, private company runs your business. That's a legitimate trade, not a mistake.
- **You don't have the time, budget, or technical help right now.** Nothing in this guide is free to act on. If moving anywhere else means learning new tools, hiring a developer, or spending a weekend you don't have, staying put and exporting your subscriber list regularly as insurance is a completely reasonable choice.

None of this is permanent. Revisit it when your list grows, your revenue changes, or something about beehiiv itself changes enough to matter.

## My verdict

> **💡 Tip — Where I land**
>
> Between these two, beehiiv is the newsletter platform I'd point a beginner toward if paid subscriptions are part of the plan — the fee structure rewards you as you grow, and the free Launch plan is a genuinely no-strings way to try newsletter writing before committing money. But "beehiiv is the better hosted platform" and "beehiiv is your permanent home" are two different claims. It's still a private, venture-funded company that could be acquired, change its pricing, or shut down features with little warning. Export your subscriber list as a CSV file every so often — beehiiv makes this simple through Settings, and it costs you nothing but a few minutes — so you're never stuck if you decide to leave.
