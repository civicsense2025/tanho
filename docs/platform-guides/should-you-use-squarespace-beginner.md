# Should you build on Squarespace? (Beginner guide)

I get asked about Squarespace a lot, usually by someone who's never touched code and just wants a website up this weekend. If that's you, this guide is for you. I'm not going to assume you know what an API is, what "hosting" means, or why anyone would care about a file format. I'm going to walk through what actually happens to your business and your content if you build on Squarespace — in plain language — and then tell you where I'd draw the line.

This is part of a bigger project I'm working on about not getting trapped by the platforms you build on. Squarespace isn't evil, and it's genuinely one of the easier ways to get a professional-looking site live fast. But "easy to start" and "easy to leave" are two very different things, and almost nobody thinks about the second one until they need it.

## What Squarespace actually is

Think of Squarespace as renting a fully furnished apartment instead of buying a house. You don't have to worry about plumbing, wiring, or a leaky roof — Squarespace handles all of that (in web terms, that's called "hosting": the computers that store your website and serve it to visitors). You pick a template, drag in your text and photos, and it looks polished almost immediately. For someone with zero technical background, that's a real, legitimate advantage.

What you get with the "rental" model:

- No server to configure — Squarespace runs the computers, you just log into a website
- No software to install — everything happens in your browser
- Nothing to "break" in a way you can't undo with the visual editor
- A polished, professional-looking template from day one

The tradeoff is the same as renting: you don't own the building. You can decorate the apartment, but you can't knock down a wall, and if the landlord changes the rules or the rent, your options are limited. In website terms, that means Squarespace controls where your site lives, how it's built under the hood, and — as I'll get into — some of the terms around what happens to your content.

## What it costs, in plain terms

> **[📸 SCREENSHOT PLACEHOLDER]** — the Squarespace pricing page at squarespace.com/pricing, showing all four plan tiers side by side

There's no free version of Squarespace. You get a 14-day free trial to build and preview a site, but it stays hidden from the public internet until you enter a credit card and pick a plan. As of my research in July 2026, here's what you're choosing between:

- **Basic — $16/month** (annual billing; about $25/month if you pay month-to-month instead). Covers a simple site: a blog, a portfolio, a small business "brochure" site with a contact form.
- **Core — $23/month** annually. This is the plan most small businesses actually need, because it's the cheapest tier that unlocks selling physical products with no extra transaction fee and lets you (or a developer you hire) add custom code to the site.
- **Plus — $39/month** annually. Adds more advanced store features like abandoned cart recovery emails and subscription products.
- **Advanced — $99/month** annually (jumping to $139/month if billed monthly). Built for stores doing serious sales volume — the main benefit is lower payment processing fees at scale.

A couple of concrete things worth knowing before you pick a plan:

- Paying annually instead of month-to-month saves you meaningfully — Squarespace says up to 28% — and annual plans also include one year of a free custom domain name (the "yourbusiness.com" part of your web address).
- If you're testing the waters, month-to-month makes sense for the first few months; once you know you're staying, switch to annual.
- If you ever want to sell things, Squarespace also takes payment processing fees on top of your subscription — typically around 2.9% + 30 cents per transaction, similar to what you'd pay with Stripe or Square directly, though Advanced-tier stores get a discount on this.

## What "you don't own your data" actually means for you

This is the part beginners skip past, and it's the part that matters most if your business ever needs to leave.

Imagine you've spent two years writing blog posts, building a customer email list, and setting up your product catalog inside Squarespace. Now imagine you want to switch to a different platform — maybe because pricing changed, maybe because you want a feature Squarespace doesn't offer, maybe because you're growing and need something more flexible. Here's what happens:

- **Your blog posts and pages**: you can export these as a single file (called XML — think of it as a plain-text container for your words and dates). But this export does not include your design, your fonts, your colors, or any of the "look" of your site. You'd essentially have your words on a napkin, not your restaurant.
- **Your photos and images**: not included in that export at all. You'd need to manually save every image yourself, one by one, before you plan to leave.
- **Certain page types don't export at all**, including gallery/portfolio pages, cover pages, and store pages — common page types for exactly the kind of small business or creative site Squarespace is best known for.
- **Your email subscriber list**: this one's better news — you can download your contacts and mailing lists as a spreadsheet (CSV) at any time, which is good, because your relationship with your audience is often the most valuable thing you own.
- **Digital products** (like a downloadable PDF guide or an online course you sell) can't be exported at all if you ever want to move your store elsewhere.

> **[📸 SCREENSHOT PLACEHOLDER]** — the "Export your site" panel inside Squarespace settings

Practically, this means: if you build your whole visual identity and store inside Squarespace and later decide to leave, you're not "moving your website" — you're rebuilding it from scratch somewhere else, using your exported text and images as raw material. That's not a dealbreaker for a lot of small sites. It is a real cost you should know about going in, not discover on the way out.

There's also fine print worth knowing about what you agree to the moment you publish anything:

- Squarespace gets a license to use, store, and display your content as part of running the service — normal for any hosting company.
- That license is broad and worldwide, and it doesn't expire even if you cancel your account.
- Squarespace also gets a separate right to feature your site in their own marketing and advertising, unless you specifically opt out in your account settings.

## Who owns Squarespace itself

> **ℹ️ Note — Who's actually running the company now**

Squarespace used to be a public company, meaning anyone could buy stock in it and its finances were reported openly every few months. That changed:

- In May 2024, the private equity firm Permira agreed to buy Squarespace for about $6.9 billion.
- After a bidding process pushed the price up, the deal closed in October 2024 at roughly $7.2 billion.
- Founder Anthony Casalena stayed on as CEO.

Practically, this means Squarespace no longer has to answer to public shareholders or explain its decisions in quarterly earnings calls — it now answers to a private equity firm focused on eventually selling the company for a profit. That's not inherently bad for you as a customer, but it's worth knowing that the incentives steering the company's roadmap changed in 2024.

## What this feels like day to day

If you're a small business owner or creative who just needs a clean site and doesn't want to think about the technical plumbing, Squarespace genuinely delivers on that promise:

- The editor is visual and forgiving — you drag, drop, and preview, with little risk of permanently breaking anything.
- Customer support exists, though several 2026 reviews note it can be slow to resolve harder issues.
- Templates look professional straight out of the box, with no design skill required to get something presentable live.

One catch worth knowing before you pick a template: if you're on the current version (called 7.1), you cannot switch to a different template later without rebuilding your site from scratch. Squarespace intentionally removed that flexibility to make the platform faster, so pick carefully at the start — this isn't something you casually change your mind about in year two.

## Data governance: how Squarespace actually handles your data

I already covered what happens to your content — your words, photos, and products — if you leave. But there's a separate, quieter question worth answering honestly: while you're still on Squarespace, who else gets access to your data, and what happens to it in situations you're probably not thinking about, like a data breach, an account closure, or Squarespace itself changing hands again?

"Data governance" is just a fancy way of asking: who's allowed to see this, where does it physically live, and what are the rules if something goes wrong?

| Question | What's actually true |
|---|---|
| Does Squarespace sell your data to strangers? | Squarespace says it doesn't "sell" your data in the plain sense. But under California's privacy law, sharing things like hashed identifiers, purchase history, and browsing activity with ad networks for targeted advertising counts as a form of "sale" or "share" — and Squarespace's own privacy policy confirms it does exactly that category of sharing to "tailor our advertising outside of the Services." You, and in some states your visitors, can opt out. |
| Where does your data physically live? | In Tier III data centers Squarespace operates in the US, plus a global network of servers (a "CDN") that speed up loading your images from whichever location is closest to each visitor. Exact addresses aren't published, which is normal for security — but it's not a single company end-to-end: parts of the infrastructure also run on outside cloud providers like AWS and Google Cloud, not just servers Squarespace built itself. |
| Is Squarespace GDPR-compliant (the EU's strict privacy law)? | Squarespace has signed the standard legal paperwork — the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, plus something called Standard Contractual Clauses — that lets it legally move European customer data to US servers. It also promises "reasonable assistance" if an EU visitor asks what data you hold on them. But making your specific site GDPR-compliant — cookie banners, consent — is still your job, not something Squarespace handles for you automatically. |
| What happens if you delete your Squarespace account? | Your account, your content, and your personal data get removed from Squarespace's systems, and Squarespace says plainly there's no recovering it afterward — no undo button. The one exception: it can keep certain data longer where the law requires it, like billing records for tax purposes. Back up everything before you hit delete, because "delete" here means delete. |
| What if Squarespace itself gets bought again, or discontinues something you rely on? | Not hypothetical — it's already happened twice recently. Squarespace bought Google's domain-registration business in 2023 after Google shut it down, absorbing roughly 10 million domains overnight. In 2024, after going private under Permira, it turned around and sold Tock — a restaurant reservation tool it had owned for three years — to American Express. If your business depends on a specific Squarespace feature, that's real recent precedent for it changing hands with little warning. |

None of this is unusually bad for a hosting company — most of it is fairly standard practice. But "standard" still means a private company you don't control decides where your data lives, who can advertise to you with it, and whether a feature you rely on sticks around. That's a real tradeoff worth knowing about, not a hypothetical one.

## Managed cloud vs. renting your own server: what leaving Squarespace actually means

If you do eventually decide to leave, it helps to understand that "leaving Squarespace" doesn't mean one single thing — there are really three different directions you could go, and they trade off very differently:

| | Staying on Squarespace | Renting your own server (self-hosted) | A more open managed option |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What this looks like** | What you have now | Software like Ghost or WordPress running on a rented computer (a "VPS") from a company like Hetzner | A stack like Vercel + Supabase — a company still runs the infrastructure, but you can take your database and code with you |
| **Who installs security updates** | Squarespace, automatically, behind the scenes | You — or someone you pay to | The platform, mostly automatically |
| **Who notices and fixes it if your site goes down** | Squarespace's team, for their own infrastructure — you still have to notice your specific site broke | Nobody but you, unless you set up your own alerts | The platform's team for infrastructure, you for anything specific to your site |
| **How predictable is the monthly bill** | Completely predictable — one flat subscription | Completely predictable — one flat server rental | Often cheap to start, but can grow if your traffic grows a lot |
| **How much of your data can you actually take with you** | Limited — see the export section above | All of it — it's your server, back it up however you like | Most of it — your database is genuinely yours, though moving still takes some effort |
| **Technical skill required** | None | Real — you're responsible for keeping a server patched and secure | Low to moderate — someone sets it up once, day-to-day is mostly hands-off |

I go deeper on the self-hosted route in my Hetzner self-hosted Postgres guides, and on the "more open managed" middle path in my Vercel + Supabase guides — both walk through the actual how-to. And if you're specifically thinking about moving off Squarespace, my migrate-from-Squarespace guide covers the mechanics of getting your content out.

The honest summary: staying on Squarespace means someone else does all the technical work, and you pay for that in flexibility and long-term ownership. Renting your own server flips that completely — full control, full responsibility. The middle path exists because most people don't actually want either extreme.

## Who should actually stay on Squarespace

I'd be doing you a disservice if this whole guide read like everyone should be racing to leave Squarespace. That's not true. Here's who I think should stay, at least for now:

- **You're still testing whether this business idea works at all.** If you don't know yet whether this website needs to exist in six months, don't spend a weekend migrating platforms for it. Squarespace is genuinely one of the fastest ways to find out if an idea has legs.
- **You have zero technical background and no budget to hire anyone.** Every alternative to Squarespace — even the "beginner-friendly" ones — asks more of you technically. If a stuck import or a confusing settings screen would completely stop you, and you can't pay someone to unstick you, staying put is the responsible choice, not the lazy one.
- **Your traffic and revenue are genuinely small right now.** The lock-in risks I've described in this guide are real, but they scale with how much content and how many customers you have. A five-page site with a trickle of visitors has very little to lose by staying. A blog with three years of posts and a growing subscriber list has a lot more.
- **You don't yet know what you'd actually want instead.** Moving off Squarespace before you know why you're leaving usually means landing somewhere else with the exact same tradeoffs, just different branding.

None of these are permanent excuses — they're honest reasons that "not yet" is a completely reasonable answer to "should I leave Squarespace?" The point of this guide isn't to talk you into leaving. It's to make sure that if you do stay, you're staying with your eyes open.

## My verdict

> **💡 Tip — Where I land**

Here's how I'd sort it, plainly:

- **Good fit**: a marketing page or simple portfolio site you're comfortable rebuilding within a year or two anyway — something where the content is short and the value is mostly the "storefront," not an archive you're building over years.
- **Not a good fit**: your primary blog, an email newsletter business, or a store you care about — anything you'd hate to rebuild from scratch, because Squarespace can't hand your content back to you in a form you can reuse elsewhere.

If you're just starting out and want speed, Squarespace can be the right first apartment. Just go in knowing it's a rental, not a house — and that the further this project goes (toward truly owning your hosting and your data, which is the whole point of what I'm building here), the more that rental arrangement will start to chafe.
